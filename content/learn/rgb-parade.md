## The plot

The parade is the RGB waveform with the three channels drawn side by side
rather than overlaid: red occupies the left third of the pane, green the
middle, blue the right. Each is a complete waveform of that channel — the
horizontal axis inside each third is still the frame's own horizontal axis.

Nothing new is being measured. What changes is that no trace is hidden
behind another, so their shapes can be compared exactly rather than
guessed at through overlap.

## What it is for

**Which channel clips first.** This is the question the parade exists to
answer, and it comes up constantly in warm light. In a sunset, a fire, a
tungsten interior or on skin in late sun, red runs out of headroom well
before the picture looks wrong. On the overlaid waveform the three traces
pile up at the top and it is genuinely hard to tell which one arrived
first. Three separate traces make it obvious.

It matters because the repair differs. If all three clip together the
highlight is simply too bright and the exposure or highlight slider is the
answer. If red alone is clipping, the fix is in colour — reducing warmth or
pulling the red channel — and lowering exposure just makes everything else
too dark.

**Whether the shadows are neutral.** Look at the *floor* of each third. If
blue's lowest point sits higher than red's and green's, the shadows are
blue, and the difference between the floors is how blue. A blue shadow with
warm highlights is one of the most common results of shooting in open
shade, and this is the fastest way to see it.

**Whether a colour cast is even.** Comparing the overall height and shape
of the three traces tells you whether a cast runs through the whole tonal
range or only part of it. Three traces of similar shape at different
heights is an even cast; three of different *shapes* is something more
complicated, and usually more interesting.

## Reading it beside the waveform

The overlaid waveform is quicker for a glance — one pane, and separation
between the traces reads immediately as a cast. The parade is the one to
switch to when the glance says something is wrong and you need to know
which channel and where.

In practice many people keep the waveform open and reach for the parade
when the answer is not obvious. Both can be on screen at once if you prefer
to have them side by side; they measure the same region and cost the same
work, because they are one measurement drawn twice.

## A note on what the widths mean

Each channel gets one third of the pane, so the picture is squeezed
horizontally by a factor of three inside each. Horizontal *positions*
remain comparable — the left of the red third is the left of the frame —
but fine horizontal detail is coarser here than in a full-width waveform.
If you are hunting for exactly which part of the frame a highlight belongs
to, the luma waveform's full width is the better instrument.
