## One line, every skin

Plot human skin on a vectorscope and it lands along a line running from the
centre out towards orange, at roughly 123 degrees on the standard
graticule. Deeper skin, paler skin, skin under warm light or cool — the
distance along the line changes, and the *angle* does not.

This is not a rule of thumb; it is a consequence of what skin is made of.
The colour of skin comes overwhelmingly from two pigments, haemoglobin and
melanin, in varying amounts against the same underlying tissue. Varying the
amount changes how far out along the line a tone sits. It does not swing it
around the circle. Broadcast engineers noticed this decades ago and put the
line on the graticule, where it has been ever since.

## How to read it

**Along the line is fine. Off the line is not.** A cloud that lies on the
line but reaches further out is more saturated skin — someone with more
colour in their face, or warmer light. That is a look, and it may be the
one you want. A cloud that sits *beside* the line is skin that has gone
green or magenta, and that essentially never looks right to anyone.

**The line is a direction, not a target.** There is no correct distance
along it. Trying to push every portrait to the same point on the line
would be as wrong as making every face the same brightness.

**It is a reference of last resort in a difficult frame.** If a portrait
has nothing neutral in it — no grey, no white, all warm wood and skin —
the skin itself becomes the thing you balance against, and this line is
what makes that possible.

## Where it fails, and it does

Being honest about this matters, because the line has a reputation for
being magic and it is not.

**Strongly coloured light on skin.** Someone lit by a neon sign, a sunset,
or bounce off a green wall genuinely *is* that colour. The line will report
them as off, correctly, and "correcting" it would remove the light in the
photograph.

**Makeup, and cosmetic colour generally.** Foundation, blusher and lipstick
are pigments chosen to sit where the wearer wants them, not where
haemoglobin does.

**Very small regions.** A face occupying a tiny part of the frame gives a
sparse trace. Select the face rather than the whole picture — measuring
skin against a background of foliage tells you about the foliage.

**Sunburn, flush and shadow.** Real variation across a real face is wide,
and the trace spreading along the line is normal.

The line is a strong prior, not a verdict. It is at its best telling you
that something has gone wrong — skin drifting off it during an edit is
almost always a mistake you made rather than a truth about the scene.

## Doing it in practice

Attach the region to the face rather than drawing a rectangle over the
whole frame, so the measurement follows as the subject moves and stays
about skin rather than about the background. Then watch the cloud's
*angle* while you adjust, not its size.

If you are matching a series of portraits, pin a patch of skin from the
frame you are happiest with and compare the rest against it with the colour
picker. Hue drift is reported separately from lightness and chroma, which
is exactly the split you want here: two frames of the same person under the
same light should differ in brightness far more readily than in hue.
