## What the indicator can show

Many rendered skin samples extend through a broadly similar orange hue region
on a vectorscope, while their level and chroma vary. The indicator makes shifts
around that direction easier to notice across a face, a sequence, or alternate
grades.

Its usefulness is comparative. If a controlled reference render places a skin
sample on one side of the indicator and another render moves it farther toward
magenta or yellow, the scope makes that hue change visible. The line does not
establish that either render is correct.

## Why there is no universal target

Complexion and undertone vary. So do illumination, surrounding bounce, makeup,
camera spectral response, input transforms, display rendering, and creative
intent. Specular highlights, lips, flushed areas, facial hair, and shadowed
skin can occupy different vectorscope positions within the same face.

Forcing all of those samples—or all people—onto one line can remove intentional
lighting and natural variation. Radial distance is also chroma in the scope's
encoding, not an independent measure of complexion or a prescribed saturation.

## A repeatable workflow

1. Choose a representative area of skin under the light you want to evaluate.
   Avoid mixed boundaries and isolated features unless they are the subject of
   the comparison.
2. Use a region or area pin large enough to reduce pixel-level variation while
   retaining the intended surface.
3. Compare the trace with a trusted reference, adjacent shots, or the approved
   look—not with the indicator alone.
4. Check the waveform or parade for channel and level behavior that the
   vectorscope omits.
5. Judge the face in context on the intended viewing display.

The same method applies to continuity: keep region choice, display path, and
reference intent consistent, then use the trace to reveal changes that visual
adaptation can make harder to notice.

The [DaVinci Resolve Colorist Guide](https://documents.blackmagicdesign.com/UserManuals/DaVinci-Resolve-20-Colorist-Guide.pdf)
likewise presents the skin-tone indicator as a guide; it is not a determinant
of an individual subject's correct rendering.
